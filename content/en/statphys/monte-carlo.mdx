## A Quantum Gas by Monte Carlo

Fermions and bosons differ in one respect: what a state is allowed to do. Two fermions may not occupy the same orbital; bosons prefer to. Everything else — the Fermi sea, the Bose condensate, the whole difference between a metal and a superfluid — follows from that.

So put it in the *move*, and nowhere else. Sample a gas of identical particles in a cubic box with Metropolis, pick a particle, propose relocating it to another orbital, and let the statistics decide which relocations exist. Then check whether Fermi–Dirac and Bose–Einstein come back out.

### The move is the physics

For **fermions**, a target orbital that is already occupied is not a legal move. There is no acceptance rule to write: the proposal simply returns nothing, and the sampler tries again. Pauli exclusion as a *shorter list of moves*.

For **bosons** the move is always legal, but it is not symmetric, and this is the part that is easy to get wrong. Metropolis assumes proposing $x \to x'$ is as likely as proposing $x' \to x$. It is not here: a target already holding $n_t$ particles is more attractive than an empty one, in proportion to $n_t + 1$. Metropolis–Hastings repairs this with a bias term,

$$
\log\frac{T(x' \to x)}{T(x \to x')} = \log\frac{n_t + 1}{n_s}
$$

where $n_s$ is the occupancy of the orbital being left. Omit it and the sampler quietly converges to the wrong distribution — not a crash, just a curve that misses. It is the one place in this library where that bias is non-zero, and it was checked against an exactly computable stationary distribution before being trusted.

The spin does the rest. A spin $s$ gives $2s+1$ states per orbital, and half-integer spin means fermions while integer means bosons — the spin–statistics theorem doing the dispatch, rather than the caller being asked the same fact twice.

### Two views, because they answer different questions

The streamed view runs **one** chain and reports a running average, so the distribution assembles itself in front of you and the error bars visibly shrink. What it cannot do is give a converged number: at any moment it is showing you a chain that has not finished.

The swept view runs **many** chains, each to convergence, in parallel processes, and plots only the endpoints. What it cannot do is show you convergence — every point arrives already settled.

A converged POST cannot show the first and a stream cannot afford the second, so both exist rather than one pretending to serve both.

### What the chemical potential is doing

$\mu$ is fixed by the requirement that the occupations add up to the particles you actually have:

$$
N = \sum_k g_k\, \bar n(\varepsilon_k), \qquad \bar n(\varepsilon) = \frac{1}{e^{(\varepsilon - \mu)/T} \pm 1}
$$

That is a monotone equation in $\mu$ with exactly one root, and solving it gives the *exact* $\mu$ drawn as a line. The *fitted* $\mu$ is what the simulation alone can offer: a least-squares fit of that same distribution to the sampled occupations. Reporting both side by side is the point — one is the answer, the other is the measurement, and only their agreement is evidence.

Two limits bracket the fermion case, and both are on the sweep:

$$
\mu \approx T_F\left[1 - \frac{\pi^2}{12}\left(\frac{T}{T_F}\right)^2\right] \quad (T \ll T_F), \qquad
\mu \approx T\log\!\left(\frac{n\lambda^3}{g}\right) \quad (T \gg T_F)
$$

The first is the Sommerfeld expansion: at low temperature $\mu$ sits at the Fermi energy and drops quadratically. The second is the classical limit, where $\mu$ goes *negative* and grows logarithmically — the gas has forgotten it is quantum. The crossover is at $T \sim T_F$, and the sweep spans it.

### Reading the live view

At **low temperature** ($T \ll T_F$) the fermion occupations are a step: one per orbital below $\mu$, zero above, with a rounding of width $\sim T$. Almost every proposed move is blocked, because almost every target is occupied, so the sampler is slow and the error bars are wide.

At **high temperature** the step washes out into an exponential tail and fermions, bosons and classical particles become indistinguishable — $\bar n \ll 1$ everywhere, so two particles rarely want the same orbital and the statistics never gets consulted.

Switch to **bosons** at low temperature and the lowest orbital starts collecting a macroscopic share of the particles. The occupation axis has no ceiling of $1$ any more, which is the entire visual difference between the two statistics.

### The honest caveats

The box is finite, so the orbital spectrum is discrete and $T_F$ is computed for exactly the orbitals present rather than from a continuum density of states.

The runs here are deliberately short — a few thousand samples — so the error bars are real and visible rather than decorative. Consecutive samples are correlated, so the true uncertainty is somewhat larger than the plotted one, which assumes independence.

And there is no condensate transition in the strict sense: Bose–Einstein condensation is a thermodynamic-limit statement, and a hundred particles in a box will show you the ground orbital filling up smoothly rather than a sharp transition at a critical temperature.
